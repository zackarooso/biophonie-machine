La Machine à Biophonie

Les Rouages

the workings — how words become music, and music a reading

You type a question. The machine does not read it the way a person would. It weighs every word on three scales of feeling, turns those weights into music, and then reads the numbers inside that music as a tarot reading. Nothing is shuffled and nothing is random: the same question, asked the same way, always makes the same music and draws the same cards. Here is the plumbing.

I

Les Mots

the weight of words

The machine carries a dictionary of feeling: the NRC Valence–Arousal–Dominance Lexicon, made by the linguist Saif M. Mohammad at the National Research Council of Canada. Many people were shown English words and asked to judge them, and their judgements were turned into three numbers for each of about twenty thousand words, each from 0 to 1:

Valence
how bright or dark a word is — pleasure against displeasure. Love sits at the very top; loss near the bottom.
Arousal
how calm or charged it is — the pulse it raises. Peace is low and still; joy is high.
Dominance
how yielding or commanding it is — being controlled against being in control. Fear yields; strong commands.

Some words are nearly opposites on one scale and close on another. Fear and hope, pictured above, are almost as far apart in brightness as words can be — yet fear is the more charged of the two, and hope the more sure of itself.

What it notices

Small words — the, will, I, and, of — carry grammar, not feeling. They are set aside from the measuring and play only softly in the music.

Turns of phrase. A negation flips the feeling of the word after it, so not happy darkens rather than brightens; a word like very strengthens what follows.

Word forms and slips. A missing word is tried by its root (hoping → hope), and a misspelt one by the nearest real word, so a slip of the hand still counts. A word the dictionary has never met simply rests at the middle.

II

La Musique

how three numbers become sound

Before a single note sounds, the machine reads the whole question once, to find its shape: where the feeling rises, where it breaks, which word is the peak of each sentence. Then it composes.

Phrases
The question is cut into short clauses — at punctuation, at joining words like and or but, and wherever the feeling leaps sharply from one word to the next. Each clause holds one chord; only the notes inside it move. A question that swings from fear to hope changes chord at the swing.
Valence → harmony and height
Brightness chooses the chord and the key: clearly bright sentences go major, clearly dark ones minor. The middle is not set at the halfway mark, because English leans bright — it sits where an ordinary question falls, so that minor can be reached at all. A passage with no clearly bright or dark word is played suspended — open chords that are neither happy nor sad. Brightness also lifts or lowers the register.
Arousal → tempo and touch
Charge sets the pace, quickening and slowing with the last few feeling-words. It also decides how each chord is played — calm words are held as still blocks, gentle ones sing a single melody, middling ones fall as a slow broken chord, charged ones flow as a quicker arpeggio.
Dominance → the singing voice
Above the chords a clear sine voice — the counter-melody — sings only on the most charged word of each stretch: the brightest or darkest, the most stirred, or the most commanding.
Ground and breath
A low bass sounds the root of each chord and rings beneath it. At punctuation the music rests, and the rest is longer when the sentence just ending was heavier with feeling.

The music is heard twice: once with nothing to look at, so that it is only sound; then again with the machine’s visuals, which move in step with each word.

III

L’Émotion

the second dictionary — and the first card

Valence, arousal and dominance describe how a word feels. A second dictionary says which feeling it carries: the NRC Word–Emotion Association Lexicon (EmoLex), by Saif M. Mohammad and Peter Turney. For thousands of words, people marked which of eight basic emotions each one calls up — anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, trust. The machine adds a ninth, calm, for the stillness the eight leave out.

Counting these across a question gives it an emotional fingerprint. Fear brings anger and fear; hope brings anticipation, joy, surprise and trust.

Twenty-two fingerprints

Each of the twenty-two Major Arcana was given a fingerprint of its own, written from the card’s traditional meaning: The Sun is mostly joy and trust; The Tower is anger, fear and surprise; The Hermit is sadness, trust and calm; The Fool is anticipation, joy, surprise and trust — the leap.

The machine compares the shape of a question’s fingerprint with all twenty-two — by the angle between them, cosine similarity, which asks how alike two shapes are whatever their size — and draws the closest. A question carried from fear toward hope, pictured above, lands on The Fool: in the old language of the cards, a step off the cliff’s edge.

When a question names too few emotions to have a fingerprint, the machine falls back on its first dictionary: each Major also has a place among brightness, charge and command, and the question’s overall tilt draws the nearest. A question so even that it leans nowhere at all lies open — and draws The Fool.

IV

Les Trois Cartes

the number · the sentiment · the hand

A reading is three cards, each drawn by a different part of what happened.

I · The Number

The Major Arcana card from the emotional fingerprint — plate III.

II · The Sentiment

A card from the music itself — the question’s feeling taken as a whole.

Suit — brightness × charge
Bright and charged is Wands (fire); bright and calm, Cups (water); dark and charged, Swords (air); dark and calm, Pentacles (earth). The lines between them were set by running many real questions through the machine, so that every suit is truly reachable.
Rank — intensity
How far the question stands, overall, from perfect neutral. That distance is divided so each rank from Ace to Ten is drawn about as often as any other.
Court — dominance
A question commanding enough brings a court figure instead of a number — Page, Knight, Queen, King as command climbs.

III · The Hand

A card from the way you ask — the one part of a reading no dictionary can know. The machine watches the asking itself: how quickly the words come, whether they are corrected, where you pause, how long the question runs. A sure, unbroken hand and a hesitant, rewritten one draw from different suits; a long question asked without a single correction may bring a court figure.

A spoken question is read from the voice in the same way — its silences and restarts. When nothing is typed at all, the sky stands in: the sun’s place in the zodiac at that moment, in one of thirty-six ten-degree decans, each ruled by one card from Two to Ten.

V

La Tradition

where the meanings come from

The numbers draw the cards; the meanings are old. The machine reads each card through the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the London circle of the 1880s and ’90s whose system underlies most tarot read today.

The Majors
Each of the twenty-two is joined to a letter of the Hebrew alphabet, to an element, planet or sign of the zodiac, and to one of the twenty-two paths on the Tree of Life of the Kabbalah. The Fool is Aleph, air, the first breath; The Star is Tzaddi, Aquarius.
The numbered cards
Each of the thirty-six from Two to Ten rules one decan of the zodiac and carries a planet in a sign, and a title — the Three of Wands is Lord of Established Strength, Sun in Aries. The Aces are the roots of their elements.
The court
Each figure is an element within an element — the Knight of Cups is Air of Water: feeling set in motion.

This is why the sky can stand in for the hand: the decans were always a calendar. The machine reaches the old correspondences from a new direction — through the measured feeling of your own words, and the music they make.

Now ask it something.
Afterwards, the machine can send you your music and your reading.

Sources. Saif M. Mohammad, “Obtaining Reliable Human Ratings of Valence, Arousal, and Dominance for 20,000 English Words,” ACL 2018 (the NRC VAD Lexicon). Saif M. Mohammad & Peter D. Turney, “Crowdsourcing a Word–Emotion Association Lexicon,” Computational Intelligence, 2013 (the NRC Emotion Lexicon).
