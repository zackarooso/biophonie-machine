La Machine à Biophonie

Comment la machine écoute

how the machine listens — the whole of it, step by step

You typed a question. The machine never read it the way a person would. It weighed each word, turned those weights into music, and then read the numbers inside that music as a tarot reading. Here is exactly how — with one real question carried all the way through.

“Will the fear I carry ever turn into hope?”

I

Les Mots

the weight of words

The machine carries a dictionary of feeling: the NRC Valence–Arousal–Dominance Lexicon, made by the linguist Saif M. Mohammad at the National Research Council of Canada. Many people were shown words and asked to judge them, and their judgements were turned into three numbers for each of 19,971 English words, each from 0 to 1:

Valence
how bright or dark the word is — pleasure against displeasure. Love is 1.00; loss is 0.06.
Arousal
how calm or charged it is — the pulse it raises. Peace is 0.16; joy is 0.82.
Dominance
how yielding or commanding it is — control against being controlled. Fear is 0.29; strong is 0.90.

What it notices

Small words — the, will, I, and, of — carry grammar, not feeling. They are set aside from the measuring and play only softly in the music.

Turns of phrase. A negation flips the feeling of the word after it, so not happy darkens rather than brightens; a word like very or so strengthens what follows.

Word forms and misspellings. If a word is missing, the machine tries its root (hoping → hope). If it is still missing and five letters or longer, it looks for the nearest real word one or two letters away, so a slip of the hand still counts.

II

La Musique

how three numbers become sound

Before a single note sounds, the machine reads the whole question once, to find its shape: where the feeling rises, where it breaks, which word is the peak of each sentence. Then it composes. Nothing is random — the same words always make the same music.

Phrases
The question is cut into short clauses: at punctuation, at joining words like and or but, after seven feeling-words, or wherever the feeling jumps sharply from one word to the next (a valence change of more than 0.30). In our question, fear at 0.07 and hope at 0.93 are so far apart that hope opens a new clause. Each clause holds one chord; only the notes inside it move.
Valence → harmony and height
Brightness chooses the chord and the key. A clearly bright sentence goes major, a clearly dark one minor. The middle is set at 0.611, not 0.5, because the English language leans bright — that is the median brightness of real questions, and without it minor would almost never sound. A clause with no clearly bright or dark word at all (every word between 0.38 and 0.74) is played suspended — open sus2 and sus4 chords that are neither happy nor sad. Brightness also lifts or lowers the register: bright clauses sit higher, dark ones lower.
Arousal → tempo and touch
Charge sets the pace: the last four feeling-words push the tempo up to 40% faster or slower. It also decides how each chord is played — calm words are held as still blocks, gentle ones sing a single melody voice, middling ones fall as a slow broken chord, charged ones flow as a quicker arpeggio.
Dominance → the singing voice
Above the chords, a clear sine voice — the counter-melody — sings only on the most charged word of each stretch — the brightest or darkest, the most stirred, or the most commanding. Here dominance has its say: a commanding word is more likely to sing. A new sentence may always sing.
Ground and breath
A low bass sounds the root of each clause’s chord and rings beneath it. At punctuation the music rests, and the rest is longer when the sentence just ending was heavier with feeling.

Small words still play — softly, a step back — so the music keeps your rhythm of speech, but they never steer it.

III

L’Émotion

the second dictionary — and the first card

Valence, arousal and dominance describe how a word feels. A second dictionary says which feeling it carries: the NRC Word–Emotion Association Lexicon (EmoLex), by Saif M. Mohammad and Peter Turney. For thousands of words, people marked which of eight basic emotions each one calls up — anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise, trust. The machine adds a ninth, calm, for the stillness the eight leave out.

Counting these across your question gives it an emotional fingerprint. In ours, fear brings anger and fear; hope brings anticipation, joy, surprise and trust.

Twenty-two fingerprints

Each of the twenty-two Major Arcana was given a fingerprint of its own, written from each card’s traditional meaning: The Sun is nearly all joy and trust; The Tower is anger, fear and surprise; The Hermit is sadness, trust and calm; The Fool is anticipation, joy, surprise and trust — the leap.

The machine compares the shape of your fingerprint with all twenty-two, by the angle between them (cosine similarity — how alike the shapes are, whatever their size), and draws the closest. Ours matched 0 · The Fool at 0.84, with The Lovers just behind. A question carried from fear toward hope is, in the old language of the cards, a step off the cliff’s edge.

If a question names fewer than two emotions, the machine falls back on its first dictionary: each Major also has a place in valence–arousal–dominance space (The Star: bright, calm, gentle; The Tower: dark, charged, overpowered), and the question’s overall tilt draws the nearest. If the question is so even that it leans nowhere at all, it lies open — and draws The Fool.

IV

Les Trois Cartes

the number · the sentiment · the hand

A reading is three cards, each drawn by a different part of what happened. None is shuffled; each is read from your question.

I · The Number

The Major Arcana card from the emotional fingerprint — plate III. For our question, The Fool.

II · The Sentiment

A card from the music itself: the averages of every feeling-word’s three numbers.

Suit — brightness × charge
Bright and charged is Wands (fire); bright and calm, Cups (water); dark and charged, Swords (air); dark and calm, Pentacles (earth). The dividing lines sit where the typical question falls — valence 0.611, arousal 0.455 — measured by running 145 real questions through the machine, so that every suit is truly reachable. Ours averaged 0.53 and 0.49: a shade darker and more stirred than most, so Swords.
Rank — intensity
How far, on average, each word stands from perfect neutral, across all three numbers at once. That distance is cut into ten bands, each holding a tenth of real questions, Ace to Ten. Ours: intensity 0.28, the seventh band — Seven.
Court — dominance
A question commanding enough brings a court figure instead of a number: Page, then Knight, Queen, King as dominance climbs. Ours, at 0.50, was not — the rank stands. Our second card: Seven of Swords.

III · The Hand

A card from the way you asked — the one part of a reading no dictionary can know.

Suit — pace × corrections
Quick (four letters a second or more) and never corrected is Wands; unhurried and never corrected, Cups; quick but rewritten, Swords; unhurried and rewritten, Pentacles.
Rank — length
The letters you typed, plus each pause and each rewrite, counted around a circle of ten. Ours: 42 letters, no pauses, no rewrites — 42 lands on Three.
Court — a long, sure hand
A long question (sixty letters or more) never once corrected brings a court figure, chosen by its pauses: none, the King; a few, the Queen; more, the Knight; many, the Page.

Our third card: Three of Wands. When a question is spoken rather than typed, the same reading is taken from the share of silence and of restarting in the voice. When nothing is typed at all, the sky stands in: the sun’s exact position in the zodiac at that moment, in one of the thirty-six ten-degree decans, each ruled by one card from Two to Ten.

V

La Tradition

where the meanings come from

The numbers draw the cards; the meanings are old. The machine reads each card through the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the London circle of the 1880s and ’90s whose system underlies most tarot read today.

The Majors
Each of the twenty-two is joined to a letter of the Hebrew alphabet, to an element, planet or sign of the zodiac, and to one of the twenty-two paths on the Tree of Life of the Kabbalah. The Fool is Aleph, air, the path from Kether to Chokmah — the first breath. The Star is Tzaddi, Aquarius.
The numbered cards
Each of the thirty-six from Two to Ten rules one ten-degree decan of the zodiac and carries a planet in a sign, and a title: the Seven of Swords is Lord of Unstable Effort, Moon in Aquarius; the Three of Wands, Lord of Established Strength, Sun in Aries. The Aces are the roots of their elements.
The court
Each figure is an element within an element — the Knight of Cups is Air of Water: feeling set in motion.

This is why the sky can stand in for the hand: the decans were always a calendar. The machine reaches the old correspondences from a new direction — through the measured feeling of your own words, and the music they made.
